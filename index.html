📋 Lesson Overview

🎯 The Big Idea

Students synthesize their learning from Lessons 3.5 (Recognizing Good Output) and 3.6 (AI Whispering Your Creativity) through structured discussion. They develop metacognitive awareness of how editorial judgment functions during the prompting process—the "back of the mind voice" that evaluates responses and guides iteration decisions. Through reflection on their field journal entries and sharing experiences with peers, students build a shared vocabulary for describing quality and understanding how context shapes AI output.

Key Conceptual Goals

By the end of this lesson, students should:

  • Articulate the connection between context decisions and output quality
  • Recognize their own developing editorial judgment as an internal evaluative voice
  • Compare quality criteria across different domains and contexts
  • Synthesize insights from individual experiences into shared understanding
  • Apply metacognitive awareness to future prompting work
  • Process their creative project experiences using the editorial framework

🔧 Teacher Preparation

Before Class Starts

Review Student Field Journals (15-20 minutes before class):

  • Scan Lesson 3.6 field journal entries from yesterday
  • Identify 3-4 strong examples that show specific criteria and metacognitive reflection (ask permission to share anonymously)
  • Note common themes or patterns across student observations
  • Identify students who may need extra support participating (prepare specific prompts for them)

Prepare Discussion Space:

  • Arrange seats to facilitate conversation (circle, U-shape, or small groups)
  • Have whiteboard/chart paper ready with three columns: Quality | Context | The Editorial Eye
  • Post discussion norms if your class uses them
  • Queue up Lesson 3.3 anchor chart (if available) for reference

Mental Preparation

This is a consolidation lesson—students are making sense of two significant concepts (context and quality) simultaneously. Your role is to:

  • Listen more than you talk (aim for 80% student voice)
  • Connect ideas students share ("That's similar to what Jamal said...")
  • Press for specificity ("Can you give an example from your journal?")
  • Validate developing understanding ("You're right that context matters differently for different topics")

⚠️ If Many Students Have Insufficient Documentation

If you discovered yesterday that many field journal entries were thin or vague, build in 5 minutes at the start of class for students to enhance their entries before discussion begins.

👨‍🏫 Detailed Facilitation Guide

Opening: Framing the Discussion (5 minutes)

OPENING SCRIPT

"Welcome back. Over the past few days, you've been building and applying crucial skills:

  • Lesson 3.5 (two days ago): You systematically developed your editorial eye—learning to evaluate AI output using specific criteria.
  • Yesterday (Lesson 3.6): You applied those skills to your creative projects, using editorial judgment to guide your work with AI.

"Today, we're connecting these experiences. Through discussion, we'll explore how context decisions and quality evaluation work together. We'll also talk about something I'm calling 'the back of the mind voice'—that internal editor that's developing as you work with AI.

"This is a Discussion Day, which means you'll do most of the talking. I'll facilitate, ask questions, and help us build on each other's ideas. Have your field journals ready—we'll be using your observations throughout."

Discussion Norms Reminder (if applicable):

  • One voice at a time
  • Build on others' ideas ("I agree with [name] because..." or "That makes me think...")
  • Use specific examples from field journals
  • It's okay to change your thinking based on discussion
  • Respect different perspectives

Activity 1: Think-Pair-Share on Quality (12 minutes)

Individual Think (3 minutes)

PROMPT

"Open your field journal to your entries from Lessons 3.5 and 3.6. Silently read through what you wrote about quality evaluation and your creative work. As you read, think about these questions:

  1. What surprised you? Did you discover something about quality evaluation that you didn't expect?
  2. What was hardest? Which aspect of judging AI output was most challenging?
  3. What got easier? How did your ability to recognize quality change?

"You have 3 minutes of silent thinking time. You might want to add notes to your journal as you reflect."

Pair Discussion (4 minutes)

TRANSITION TO PAIRS

"Now turn to a partner. Take turns sharing:

  • ONE surprising discovery from your evaluation work
  • ONE example of a quality difference you noticed

"Each person gets about 2 minutes. I'll give you a heads-up when it's time to switch."

Whole-Class Share (5 minutes)

SYNTHESIS PROTOCOL

"Let's hear from several pairs. I'm looking for surprising discoveries about quality evaluation. When you share, name the specific criterion or observation from your journal."

Capture insights on the board under "Quality" column. Use students' own words. Look for patterns.

Common Themes to Listen For:

  • Quality criteria vary by topic/audience
  • Small changes (word choice, sentence length) have big impacts
  • Evaluation is easier with systematic criteria vs. gut reactions
  • Different domains prioritize different criteria
  • "Good enough" depends on purpose

Activity 2: Context Revisited (10 minutes)

BRIDGING SCRIPT

"We've been talking about quality—what makes output effective. Now let's connect back to Lesson 3.3, where we explored how context decisions affect AI responses.

"Here's my question for you: When you were evaluating AI responses or working on your creative project, did the CONTEXT of your prompt affect what you considered 'good' output?

"Think-pair-share again, but this time focusing on the connection between context and quality."

Think-Pair-Share Round 2 (6 minutes total)

  • Individual Think (1 min): How did context decisions affect what counted as "good"?
  • Pair Discussion (2 min): Share examples where context mattered
  • Whole-Class Discussion (3 min): Build shared understanding

Key Insights to Surface:

  1. Audience affects quality criteria - Expert audience = complexity okay; Beginner audience = simplicity essential
  2. Purpose shapes evaluation - Quick overview = brevity valued; Comprehensive guide = completeness essential
  3. Domain knowledge changes judgment - Topics you know well = easier to spot errors or gaps
  4. Context precision improves output - Clear context = easier to evaluate fit

Synthesis Question: "So if context affects quality... what does this mean for how you prompt AI?"

Capture key ideas under "Context" column on board.

Activity 3: The Editorial Voice (15 minutes)

This is the heart of the lesson—developing metacognitive awareness of editorial judgment.

INTRODUCING THE CONCEPT

"I want to introduce a concept: the 'back of the mind voice.'

"When you were working in Lessons 3.5 and 3.6, evaluating AI responses and developing your stories, you were building something in your brain—a kind of internal editor. This voice looks at output and makes quick judgments: That's clear. That's confusing. That needs more detail. That's perfect.

"This voice develops through practice. The more you evaluate AI output with specific criteria, the faster and more automatic this judgment becomes. Eventually, it happens while you're prompting—you read a response and IMMEDIATELY know whether it's good enough or whether you need to iterate.

"This is what professional editors do. They've developed this voice so strongly that they can spot problems instantly. You're building the same skill, but for AI output.

"Let's explore this together."

Round 1: Recognizing the Voice (4 minutes)

PROMPT

"Think about Lessons 3.5 and 3.6. When you read AI responses, did you have immediate reactions? Like reading the first sentence and thinking 'oh, this one's good' or 'this one's confusing'?

"Turn and talk with your partner: When did you notice yourself making quick quality judgments?"

After pairs discuss, take 5-6 examples from whole class. Validate these experiences:

  • "That's your editorial voice developing"
  • "You're noticing quality patterns automatically"
  • "That quick judgment comes from systematic practice"

Round 2: When the Voice Guides Action (4 minutes)

PROMPT

"Now think about yesterday's creative work. You prompted AI for a story element. AI gave you a response.

"Your 'back of the mind voice' read it and said: 'This is bland. It needs more personality.'

"What do you DO with that judgment? How does recognizing 'this needs more personality' help you iterate?"

Key Insight to Surface:

The editorial voice does two things:

  1. EVALUATES: "This works" or "This needs improvement"
  2. DIRECTS: "It needs more personality" tells you HOW to iterate—"Add more specific personality traits"

This is why editorial judgment is so powerful—it doesn't just say "good" or "bad," it tells you what to DO next.

Round 3: Developing the Voice (4 minutes)

PROMPT

"Final question: Based on your experience, what helps this editorial voice develop? What made you better at recognizing quality?"

Expected Student Observations:

  • Practice with systematic criteria
  • Comparing multiple responses
  • Documenting specific features
  • Explaining reasoning to teammates
  • Getting feedback from others

Capture key ideas under "The Editorial Eye" column.

Synthesis Moment:

"So here's what we've discovered today:

  • Quality evaluation isn't just 'I like this one better.' It's systematic judgment using specific criteria.
  • Context decisions shape what counts as 'good'—audience, purpose, and domain all matter.
  • The editorial voice develops through practice and guides your iteration. It evaluates AND directs.

"This voice is now part of how you interact with AI. Every time you prompt, this voice is working in the background, helping you decide: Is this good enough, or should I iterate?"

Activity 4: Creative Project Check-In (5 minutes)

CREATIVE PROJECT BRIDGE

"Many of you worked on your creative projects yesterday. Let's connect today's discussion to that work.

"Think about yesterday's session. When you read what AI generated, did you use your editorial voice? Did you think things like:

  • 'This character feels flat—needs more depth'
  • 'This dialogue doesn't sound natural'
  • 'This setting description is too generic'

"Quick popcorn share—call out examples of your editorial voice at work."

Take 5-8 quick examples.

Then ask: "How many of you noticed your editorial voice getting STRONGER as you worked? Like you're getting faster at recognizing what needs improvement?"

Acknowledge growth: "That's the skill compounding. The more you practice evaluation, the better you get."

Closing: Synthesis and Field Journal (3 minutes)

CLOSING SYNTHESIS

"Today we've connected three big ideas:

  1. Quality evaluation uses systematic criteria—not just personal taste
  2. Context decisions affect what 'good' means—audience, purpose, domain all matter
  3. Your editorial voice guides iteration—it evaluates AND directs improvement

"Tomorrow in Lesson 3.8, you'll put this editorial judgment to work building simulations. Your editorial voice will be your guide."

Field Journal Prompt (write on board):

"Describe your 'back of the mind voice.' When do you hear it most clearly? What does it help you notice? How has it changed since you started Chapter 3?"

OR

"Choose one insight from today's discussion that changed how you think about prompting AI. What was the insight? Why does it matter?"

Students have 2-3 minutes to write.

For Advanced Students

🚧 Anticipated Challenges & Solutions

Challenge 1: Students Have Insufficient Field Journal Documentation

Solution: Build in 5 minutes for journal enhancement before discussions. Display anonymous strong examples. Focus think-pair-share on what they REMEMBER even if not documented.

Challenge 2: Discussion Stays Surface-Level

Solution: Press consistently for specificity: "Show me the journal entry." Model going deeper. Use "say more" prompt. Redirect to evidence.

Challenge 3: Dominant Voices, Quiet Voices

Solution: Use talking chips. Structured turn-taking. Think-pair-share increases participation. "Phone a friend" for quiet students.

Challenge 4: Students Can't Connect Context and Quality

Solution: Use concrete examples. Display anchor charts. Draw two-column chart showing connections. Work through one example together.

Challenge 5: "Editorial Voice" Concept Feels Abstract

Solution: Use multiple metaphors. Ask about other domains ("When you watch a movie, do you notice bad acting?"). Make it physical. Focus on examples from their experience.
