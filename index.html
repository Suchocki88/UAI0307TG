📋 Lesson Overview

⚠️ This Is a Different Kind of Lesson

Lesson 3.7 shifts from skill-building to critical consciousness. Students discover that the evaluation work they performed in Lesson 3.5 is the actual labor that thousands of people do professionally for low wages globally. This revelation can be uncomfortable, provocative, and deeply engaging.

Be prepared for: Strong emotional reactions, ethical complexity, challenging questions about responsibility and exploitation, and discussions that may extend beyond one class period.

The Big Idea

Students learn the formula "AI = Mathematics + Human Labor" and explore both dimensions:

  • Global Implications: The invisible workforce behind AI, working conditions, wages, economic exploitation, and the irony of workers training their own replacements
  • Personal Implications: How students' developing editorial judgment connects to this labor, and what it means to be an informed, ethical AI user

Key Learning Goals

By the end of this lesson (which may span 1-2 class periods), students should be able to:

  • Explain the role of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) in AI development
  • Describe working conditions and economic realities for global RLHF workforce
  • Articulate ethical questions about AI labor practices
  • Connect their own editorial judgment skills to professional RLHF work
  • Recognize that technology development has winners and losers
  • Understand whose labor and whose values shape AI systems

This Lesson in Context

✅ What Students Already Know:

  • Lesson 3.5: Systematic evaluation of AI outputs using five criteria (clarity, engagement, accuracy, completeness, audience fit)
  • Lesson 3.6: Applied editorial judgment to their creative writing projects
  • They've experienced firsthand what evaluation work feels like
  • They've developed their "back of the mind voice" for assessing quality

🔮 What This Lesson Prepares Students For:

  • Chapter 4 (AI Bias): Direct connection—whose labor shapes AI determines whose perspectives dominate
  • Chapter 6 (Labor and AI): Deep dive into these themes
  • Informed AI Use: Students will never interact with AI the same way again

💡 How to Decide: One Day or Two?

Choose two days if:

  • Students are deeply engaged with Round 2 discussion questions (ethics, responsibility, exploitation)
  • Multiple students want to contribute and you're having to cut discussion short
  • The emotional/intellectual weight feels significant and students need processing time
  • You have students who would benefit from extended reflection time

Choose one day if:

  • Discussion reaches natural conclusions within timeframe
  • Students seem ready to move forward
  • You're tight on schedule and need to keep pacing

Remember: Part 2 topics (personal implications) can always be addressed through field journal homework or woven into subsequent lessons. The critical content is Part 1—the RLHF revelation.

🔧 Preparation

Before Class

  • Read the student-facing lesson completely so you understand the RLHF information and discussion questions
  • Decide your approach: Will you plan for one day or be prepared to extend to two?
  • Consider your students: Who might have strong reactions? Who might need additional support processing this information?
  • Prepare yourself emotionally: This content can feel heavy. Be ready to hold space for discomfort, anger, sadness, or defensiveness
  • Optional: Research additional current examples of RLHF labor practices if you want more specifics beyond what's in the lesson

Materials Needed

  • Student-facing Lesson 3.7 guide (digital or printed)
  • Students' field journals
  • Whiteboard/chart paper for capturing discussion themes (optional but recommended)
  • Timer for managing discussion rounds

🎯 Your Role Today

You're facilitating difficult conversations about labor, ethics, and technology. Your job is to:

  • Present information clearly without editorializing
  • Hold space for complexity—resist the urge to provide easy answers
  • Validate all perspectives while pushing for evidence-based reasoning
  • Keep discussions productive rather than spiraling into despair or dismissiveness
  • Help students sit with discomfort rather than rushing to resolution

📍 Detailed Lesson Flow

Opening: Framing the Lesson (5 minutes)

Suggested Opening Script

"Welcome. Today's lesson is different from what we've been doing. Over the past two days, you evaluated AI outputs in Lesson 3.5 and applied those skills to your creative projects in 3.6. You developed your 'editorial eye'—the ability to recognize good output and direct AI effectively."

"Today, you're going to learn something that will change how you think about every AI interaction from now on. You're going to discover who does the work that makes AI 'intelligent,' under what conditions, and what that means for all of us."

"This content can be uncomfortable. That's okay—it should be. We're going to explore some difficult ethical questions together, and I don't expect us to solve them. What I do expect is that you engage honestly and thoughtfully."

"For the next 10 minutes, I want you to read Sections B through F in your lesson guide independently. Read carefully. Take notes if you want. When you're done, we'll discuss."

⚠️ Critical Teaching Move: Give Reading Time

Do NOT skip the independent reading time. Students need to absorb the RLHF information at their own pace before discussion begins.

What this looks like:

  • Students read silently for 8-10 minutes
  • Teacher circulates quietly, available for clarifying questions only
  • Allow processing time—some students will finish before others
  • Watch for reactions: surprise, confusion, concern

Why this matters: The revelation that "you just did real AI work" hits harder when students discover it themselves through reading than when told by the teacher. The written format also provides concrete facts (wages, working conditions, scale) that ground the subsequent discussion.

Transition to Discussion (2 minutes)

After Reading Time

"Okay, put down your reading. Take a breath. I imagine some of you are surprised by what you just learned. Maybe confused. Maybe uncomfortable. Maybe skeptical. All of those reactions are valid."

"We're going to unpack this together through three rounds of discussion. First, we'll share initial reactions. Then we'll dig into the ethics and economics. Finally, we'll think about what this means moving forward."

"Let's start with Round 1."

Round 1: Initial Reactions (5-7 minutes)

Individual Reflection (2 minutes)

Prompt students: "Take 2 minutes to think and maybe jot notes in your field journal: What surprised you most about what you just read? How does this change how you think about AI?"

Pair Discussion (3-5 minutes)

Instructions: "Turn to a partner and share your reactions. What surprised you? What did you have similar responses to? What did you see differently?"

Teacher during pair time: Circulate and listen. Note themes emerging. Identify students who might contribute specific insights to whole-class discussion.

Whole-Class Sharing (Optional, if time)

Invite 2-3 pairs to share one surprising thing they discussed. Keep this brief—the goal is to surface initial reactions, not solve problems yet.

Round 2: Ethics & Economics (8-10 minutes)

💡 This Is the Core Discussion

This round digs into the hard questions. Choose 3-4 questions from the lesson guide based on your students' interests and your time. You don't need to cover all six.

Recommended focus questions:

  • Question 1 (Is this exploitation?): Great for establishing definitions and criteria
  • Question 2 (Who's responsible?): Moves beyond individual blame to systemic analysis
  • Question 4 (The replacement paradox): Unique to AI labor—connects to broader automation anxieties

Facilitation Strategies

If Students Say... You Might Respond...
"This is definitely exploitation" "What makes labor exploitative? What criteria are you using?" Push for specifics.
"But they chose these jobs" "Does choice always equal fairness? What if this is someone's best option—does that change anything?"
"AI companies should just pay more" "Who else benefits from keeping costs low? What would happen to 'free' AI tools if labor costs increased?"
"This isn't our fault as users" "What responsibility, if any, do users have? Can individual choices affect systems this large?"
"I feel guilty now about using AI" "Guilt isn't the goal—awareness is. What does informed, ethical AI use look like to you?"
"This is just like factory work/fast food" "What's similar? What's different? Is training your replacement a unique dimension?"

⚠️ Avoid These Pitfalls

  • Lecturing about what students "should" think: Present facts, facilitate reasoning, but don't prescribe conclusions
  • Allowing discussion to become US vs. Them: This isn't about demonizing AI companies—it's about understanding systems
  • Rushing to solutions: Sit with the complexity. Not everything has a clear answer
  • Dismissing student discomfort: Validate it, explore where it comes from, use it as a learning tool

Round 3: Looking Forward (5-8 minutes)

This round helps students process what they've learned and think about implications:

Discussion Questions (Choose 1-2)

  1. Automation paradox: "AI is supposed to reduce human labor, but currently requires massive human labor. What's happening here?"
  2. Your skills in context: "You're developing editorial judgment that has economic value. How does knowing about RLHF change how you think about your learning?"
  3. Whose voices matter?: "If most RLHF workers are in specific countries earning low wages, whose values and perspectives dominate AI?"

Bridge to Chapter 4: "That last question—whose voices matter—is exactly what we'll explore in our next chapter on AI Bias. The labor practices we discussed today directly shape whose perspectives AI considers 'correct' or 'good.'"

Field Journal Reflection (10 minutes)

Choose the Right Prompt

One-day approach: Use Option 1 (Global Focus). Students write about RLHF labor, ethics, and responsibility.

Two-day approach:

  • Day 1: Option 1 (Global Focus)
  • Day 2: Option 2 (Integrated Focus) connecting global and personal dimensions

During writing time: Circulate quietly. This is reflective work—let students process independently. Note who's engaging deeply vs. struggling to engage.

Closing (3-5 minutes)

Suggested Closing

"Today was heavy. We explored difficult questions about labor, exploitation, and responsibility. Many of those questions don't have clear answers—and that's okay."

"What matters is that you now know something most people don't: AI = Mathematics + Human Labor. Every time you interact with AI from now on, you'll carry that knowledge."

"[If one day] We didn't get to Part 2 today—the personal implications topics. Those are in your lesson guide, and we may circle back to some of them in future lessons or you can reflect on them in your field journal."

"[If two days] Tomorrow we'll connect what we learned today to your own skill development—your editorial judgment, your creative work, your 'back of the mind voice.'"

"Thank you for engaging thoughtfully with difficult material. See you tomorrow."

🚧 Anticipated Challenges & Solutions

Challenge: "This is too depressing"

Response: "It's heavy, yes. But awareness isn't the same as hopelessness. What does informed action look like? What power do you have—even if limited?"

Reframe: Critical consciousness about systems is empowering, not paralyzing. Understanding how things work is the first step toward change.

Challenge: "I don't want to feel guilty about using AI"

Response: "The goal isn't guilt—it's awareness. You can use AI AND understand the labor behind it. What does ethical use look like to you?"

Reframe: Individual guilt is unproductive. Systemic awareness enables better choices and advocacy for change.

Challenge: "But these workers chose these jobs"

Response: "True. Does choice always equal fairness? What if someone's choices are constrained by circumstances? Does that change anything?"

Push thinking: Explore the difference between "choice" and "meaningful choice." What conditions need to exist for work to be chosen freely vs. out of necessity?

Challenge: "This is just capitalism/This is just how global economy works"

Response: "That may be descriptively true—this IS how things currently work. Does that make it right? Are there alternative models?"

Avoid: Getting into political debates about capitalism vs. other systems. Focus on specific labor practices and what "fair" might look like within current or alternative systems.

Challenge: Students want to "solve" the problem immediately

Response: "I appreciate the desire to fix this. But first, we need to understand it fully. What information would we need to design better systems?"

Validate and redirect: Solution-seeking is good, but premature solutions often miss complexity. Deepen understanding first.

Challenge: Discussion becomes unproductive or off-topic

Redirect: "That's an interesting tangent. Let's bookmark it and come back to our central question: [restate discussion question]."

Use discussion questions as anchors: Keep returning to the specific prompts in the lesson guide.

Challenge: Dominant voices overwhelming quieter students

Strategy: Use Think-Pair-Share format consistently. Everyone processes individually first, shares with partner, THEN whole-class.

Call on strategically: "I want to hear from someone who hasn't shared yet. [Name], what's your thinking?"

💭 Final Notes for Teachers

This Lesson Plants Seeds

Students won't "finish" processing this content in one or two class periods. The revelation that AI requires massive human labor will continue to inform their thinking throughout the course and beyond.

Expect:

  • Students bringing up RLHF in future lessons
  • Questions about whose labor shaped specific AI capabilities
  • Heightened awareness during Chapter 4 (Bias) about whose perspectives dominate
  • Changed relationships with AI tools—more critical, more questioning

This is good. This is the critical consciousness you're cultivating.

Take Care of Yourself

Facilitating discussions about exploitation, economic inequality, and labor injustice can be emotionally taxing—especially if you're teaching multiple sections of this lesson in one day.

Remember to:

  • Take breaks between classes if possible
  • Process your own reactions to student discussions
  • Recognize that holding space for discomfort is valuable work
  • Connect with colleagues about difficult conversations

Why This Lesson Matters

In a course about AI literacy, this might be the most important lesson. Not because RLHF is the most technically complex concept, but because it reveals the human reality behind technological "magic."

Students who complete this lesson will:

  • Never use AI uncritically again
  • Ask better questions about who benefits from technology
  • Understand that every tool has costs—visible and invisible
  • Recognize their own agency as informed users and future citizens
  • Connect technology to labor, economics, and power structures

This is AI literacy that matters.
